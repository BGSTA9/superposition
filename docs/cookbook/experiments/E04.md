# E04 · nonuniform_geometry: polytopes that deform, snap and collapse

## 1. Claim and pages

**The paper's claims (pp. 21–25).** E03's features were all alike. Here they are not.

- **(a) Perturbing one feature (p. 22).** Five features in m = 2 at density 0.05 form a
  pentagon. Make one feature denser and its neighbours move away; make it sparser and they
  close in. Past about 2.5× sparser, the pentagon snaps to two digons with that feature
  dropped, where the two geometries' loss curves cross.
- **(b) Correlated and anticorrelated sets in m = 2 (pp. 23–24).** Features of a *correlated
  set* are zero or nonzero together; an *anticorrelated set* never has two members active at
  once. Correlated features prefer to be orthogonal, and side by side when there is no room.
  Anticorrelated features prefer to be antipodal.
- **(c) Local almost-orthogonal bases (pp. 24–25).** In a larger model, each correlated set
  forms its own nearly orthogonal basis.
- **(d) Collapse to principal components (p. 25).** Three correlated pairs in m = 2 form a
  hexagon with pair members adjacent when very sparse, and collapse pair by pair onto
  (a + b)/√2 as density rises.

The paper states no density for (b)–(d). ([SPEC.md](../../../SPEC.md) §7 E04; D-307;
[REPRODUCTION.md](../../REPRODUCTION.md) § E04.) **[Documented]**

### 4b. Doing it by hand

**[Recommended]** for every step below.

1. **Build and test the two set distributions.** Correlated: one switch per set, independent
   U[0, 1] values. Anticorrelated: at most one member active.
   - Command for the repo's tests: `uv run pytest tests/unit/test_data.py -q -k correlated`.
   - Check: in 100,000 samples, count co-activations. Correlated members always co-occur;
     anticorrelated members never do.
2. **(a) Sweep the perturbed feature and keep every restart.** Put the perturbed feature at
   index 0 so canonicalization puts it on top, as on p. 22 (D-307). Classify each restart's
   geometry (pentagon, or digons with feature 0 dropped).
   - Expected: the best restart is two digons up to a ratio of about 0.25 and a pentagon from
     about 0.32 on; the neighbour angle grows from about 56° to about 76°.
   - Check: plot the lowest loss of each geometry per ratio. The two curves must cross; that
     crossing is the first-order transition.
3. **Size the evaluation to the rarest event you need to rank.** Count how often a set is
   active in your evaluation batch at its density. If it is a few hundred, your loss
   differences of a few percent are noise; raise the sample count before you compare restarts.
4. **Grade trends with a rank statistic, and guard it.** Spearman ρ between ratio and angle,
   computed on the best *pentagon* per ratio (p. 22 describes the pentagon deforming before it
   snaps).
   - Check: Spearman of a constant series must come back NaN, not a number
     (`test_e04_spearman_matches_scipy_and_rejects_constant_input`).
5. **(b)–(d): write the paper's sentences as checks before training.** For example "side by
   side" = all six represented and each pair's members angular neighbours.
   - Then ask whether each check could pass for the wrong reason. The anticorrelated check
     passes with any negative cosine (R2-09). Add the stronger statement you actually believe
     (cos ≈ −1) as INFO.
6. **Before you report a discrepancy with a figure, read the figure's data, not its caption.**
   Render the page at 300 dpi and read the crossing point off the plot (the R2-04 lesson).

## 6. What actually happened

1. **Settings the paper does not state were chosen from its figure.** Density 0.02 for (b) and
   (c) sits in p. 25's "no features collapsed" regime; (d)'s sweep spans every regime it names
   (D-307). **[Documented]**
2. **A change made before the cited run, after a smoke run (flagged).** The 200,000-sample
   selection for (b)–(d) was committed in `230a51d` minutes after an evaluation of those parts
   on 200,000 samples [S 5ab2965c @ 2026-09-30T07:56Z]. No threshold changed: the thresholds
   in `configs/nonuniform_geometry.yaml` are identical at `1fb44c4` and today; the diff adds
   only `eval_samples` and runtime comments. **[Documented]**
3. **A lenient criterion, stated openly.** "Lower mean cosine than the correlated sets" passes
   with any negative within-pair cosine. The best restart is exactly antipodal (loss about
   10⁻¹⁶), but only 5 of 16 restarts find it; the other 11 keep each pair orthogonal. The two
   setups also differ in density: 0.01 per anticorrelated feature against 0.02 per correlated
   one (R2-09; REPRODUCTION.md § E04). **[Documented]**
4. **The (d) regimes are shifted.** Ours: one pair collapsed at 0.085–0.121, two at 0.172–0.35,
   all three at 0.5; the paper's: 0.05–0.15, 0.15–0.2, 0.25–0.5. At 0.5 our collapsed
   features have norm 0.65, below the paper's 1/√2. Open, not graded. **[Documented]**
5. **A per-feature loss match is the reviewer's reading, not a run's.** The reviewer found our
   best losses divided by n = 5 match the p. 22 curves (for example 1.7e-3 for the pentagon at
   1×). It was read off the figure, not computed in a run (R2-04). **[Documented]**
6. **Decisions:** D-109, D-307, D-309 in [DECISIONS.md](../../DECISIONS.md). **[Documented]**

## 9. Explain it back

1. Why does making feature 0 denser push its neighbours away? Think about which interference
   terms become more frequent.
2. Two anticorrelated features are never active together. Why does that make an antipodal pair
   cost nothing, and why do 11 of 16 restarts still miss it?
3. Why, as density rises, do correlated pairs collapse onto (a + b)/√2 rather than keep two
   directions? What does that have in common with PCA?
